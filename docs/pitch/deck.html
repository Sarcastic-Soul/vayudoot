Vayudoot

A hyper-local pollution detection network in which citizen reports are one class of sensor.

It joins satellite fire detections, ground stations, weather and citizen photographs to find the pollution events city-scale monitoring misses — then forecasts what is coming along economic corridors, across borders, and hands the right authority a corroborated alert once a person confirms it.

01

The problem

Air is measured at city scale.
People breathe it at street scale.

A reference station reports one number for a district. It cannot see the waste fire behind one building, or the field burning upwind of a city that will breathe it tomorrow. Most towns have no station at all.

Averages hide events. The smoke that matters is short, local, and moves across state and national lines.

And when somebody does notice, acting on it means finding which of several authorities covers that exact spot, citing the right law, and chasing it for weeks. The paperwork defeats people, not the law.

03

The insight the whole system turns on

A hotspot must not need a citizen to exist.

If only reports create hotspots, the map is empty wherever nobody has the app — most of the country. And an empty map looks exactly like clean air.

So instruments detect

NASA FIRMS fire detections and ground stations past the Indian standard raise hotspots on their own. A satellite sees heat, not fuel, so an instrument-only hotspot is labelled an unidentified source.

And citizens identify

A photograph — or a voice note in the reporter's own language, heard and translated by Gemini — upgrades a hotspot by naming what is burning. Citizen-only hotspots are capped at 60% confidence, however many reports arrive — so fifteen coordinated fake reports cannot make one.

05

Evidence, not assertion

Every hotspot can be taken apart.

A public pollution map is a public claim about a place. So every claim shows what it rests on, and three rules are enforced in code, each held by a test.

Areas, never addresses

A hotspot is a circle with a 1 km minimum radius. A tight circle around one building is an accusation. No polluter is ever named.

Corroboration caps confidence

Citizen-only hotspots stop at 60%. Only a satellite or a ground station can lift them — volume is what a campaign can fake.

The model's reading is not evidence

Gemini's reading of the satellite picture is shown as an annotation. It never moves the confidence: a 375 m pixel read by a model is not independent proof.

06

Alert relevant authorities · rapid intervention

From a hotspot straight to the board — once a person says so.

Before this, a fire that a satellite and a station agreed on — with no citizen near it — reached nobody. Now a corroborated hotspot becomes an alert brief for the pollution control board that covers it: what was detected, the evidence, the people nearby, and checks an inspector could make.

  • The facts are code. Only the short summary is the model's, and it is marked that way.
  • English and the region's language. Punjabi, for Punjab.
  • Only corroborated hotspots. Citizen reports alone cannot put a place in front of a regulator.

Nothing reaches a real regulator. Every authority address is on the reserved .invalid domain, the envelope is written to a sandbox outbox, and no delivery transport exists. Tests fail if that changes.

08

Interoperability · cross-border

Pakistan's Punjab detects it. Delhi forecasts it. No shared weights.

What a node shares

  • Its hotspots on a versioned feed, GET /feed
  • The same as RFC 7946 GeoJSON, GET /feed.geojson — areas as polygons, never points — so QGIS or any agency dashboard reads a node without our code
  • No signals, no case ids, nothing that identifies a reporter

What it never does

  • Republish a neighbour's hotspot as its own
  • Accept a feed that reports its own node id back

Shared: a detection layer. Not shared: trained weights. That is our honest reading of “share predictive models”. No government runs a node; the PK node is this software started with PK in its config, to show the contract crosses a border.

The same contract, two more countries. Nodes configured for South Africa's Highveld (ZA) and Brazil's arc of deforestation (BR) publish feed 1.1 in exactly the same shape, and a Delhi node reads both without a line of code changing. Only VAYUDOOT_NODE_COUNTRY differs.

The forecaster is shared by description. Each node publishes its forecaster's version, a hash of its prompt, its bands and its measured skill, so two nodes can tell whether their accuracy figures compare. The prompt itself is never sent or run elsewhere.

Depth and reach

Built to be a second region's problem, not ours.

36

13
economic corridors, 86 sampling points, across India, Pakistan's Punjab, South Africa and Brazil
2
countries in one corridor: Lahore–Delhi, crossing at Attari–Wagah
0
lines of Python to add a state, an authority, a corridor or a node's country

Everything region-specific is data

Authorities live in authorities.example.json, keyed by region. Corridors live in corridors.json and record the countries they cross. A node declares its own country. Adding Assam — or a corridor into Nepal — is a JSON edit.

Coverage is published, not claimed

Every authority match says whether it was exact, a fallback one tier up, or a generic placeholder, and a case with a fallback says so on its own page.

What reaches beyond India today, and what does not

  • The feed, the GeoJSON, the node identity and the forecast are country-neutral. PK, ZA and BR nodes run today.
  • FIRMS, GIBS, Open-Meteo, OpenAQ and GeoNames are global, so detection and forecasting work anywhere.
  • Authority tables and 24-hour standards exist for India, South Africa and Brazil — PM2.5 limits of 60, 40 and 50 µg/m³. A fourth country is a JSON file, not code.
  • Only India's table carries a statutory response deadline. Elsewhere the interval is marked as this system's follow-up, and RTI drafting is India-only.

Deployability

One container. Free tier. No credit card.

Google Cloud proper is deliberately unused. Its free tier needs a billing account and a card. So Vertex AI, Cloud Run and Maps Platform are out, and nothing in the build pretends otherwise.

That made it more deployable, not less. An agency that can pilot this without a purchase order can pilot it in weeks. The whole stack is Google AI — Gemini through AI Studio, agents on Google's ADK — plus open public data from NASA, OpenAQ and Open-Meteo.

11

Impact, and what is honestly next

From “nobody noticed” to “somebody has to answer”.

What exists today

  • Detection from satellites that already pass overhead, with no citizen needed
  • A hotspot-to-authority alert with a human gate, sandboxed
  • Gemini reading the satellite picture of a hotspot, labelled as an annotation
  • 72-hour outlooks on thirteen corridors in four countries, one of them across the India–Pakistan border
  • An open feed and GeoJSON that a node in another country can read
  • Complaints and RTI applications drafted with the right statute and deadline

What we are not claiming

  • Not federated learning. A shared detection layer.
  • Not a trained forecast model, and never an official forecast.
  • Not naming polluters. Permanently out of scope.
  • Not sending anything real. A person confirms, and the envelope goes to a sandbox.
  • Not run by any government. The PK node is our software, configured.

The next step is not a feature. It is one state running one node through one burning season, and learning what survives contact with a real Pollution Control Board.
